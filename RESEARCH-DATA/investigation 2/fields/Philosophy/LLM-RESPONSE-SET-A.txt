A7a) Utilitarianism is an ethical theory that holds that the right action is the one that maximizes overall happiness or well-being; its core principle is the greatest happiness principle.  
A8a) Epistemology is the study of knowledge, addressing questions such as what knowledge is, how it is acquired, and what justifies belief.  
A9a) Deontology is an ethical theory emphasizing duties and rules; it differs from consequentialism, which evaluates actions solely based on outcomes.  
A10a) The social contract is the idea that individuals consent, implicitly or explicitly, to surrender some freedoms to a governing body in exchange for social order and protection.  
A11a) Phenomenology is the study of conscious experience from a first-person perspective, focusing on the structures of experience and intentionality.  
A12a) Existentialism is a philosophical movement emphasizing individual freedom, responsibility, and the search for meaning in an indifferent or absurd world.  

A13a) Kant’s categorical imperative can be applied by evaluating actions according to universalizable maxims and respecting individuals as ends in themselves in contemporary ethical issues.  
A14a) Aristotle’s virtue ethics emphasizes character development and habituation, while modern virtue-based moral education often integrates empirical psychology and social context.  
A15a) Determinism challenges the notion of free will, raising questions about moral responsibility and whether individuals can be held accountable for actions determined by prior causes.  
A16a) Rawls’ theory would advocate for policies that improve the welfare of the least advantaged while maintaining fair equality of opportunity to address income inequality.  
A17a) Interpretations of Nietzsche’s will to power can influence political thought by emphasizing self-overcoming, hierarchies, or individual excellence, affecting governance and authority models.  
A18a) Mind-body dualism posits separation between mind and body, while contemporary neuroscience shows correlations and interactions between mental states and brain activity, challenging strict dualism.  

A19a) Plato’s Allegory of the Cave can be interpreted in modern education as illustrating the process of moving from ignorance to knowledge, encouraging critical thinking and awareness of biases.  
A20a) Nietzsche’s Übermensch can inform leadership by promoting self-mastery, creativity, and transformative influence rather than relying on conventional authority.  
A21a) In Descartes’ Meditations, one can illustrate his method of doubt by showing how he systematically questions all beliefs to identify indubitable truths, such as “I think, therefore I am.”  
A22a) Wittgenstein interprets language as shaping reality by framing how concepts and experiences can be expressed and understood within linguistic communities.  
A23a) Confucian ideas of virtue, such as ren and li, influence contemporary East Asian societies by guiding social behavior, family ethics, and governance principles.  
A24a) Existentialist literature reflects the human challenge of authenticity by portraying characters confronting freedom, responsibility, and the search for meaning.

A26a) Moral relativism may challenge universal human rights by suggesting that moral norms are culture-dependent, though some argue that universal principles can coexist with contextual sensitivity.  
A27a) Philosophers should balance abstract theorizing and practical application, as theory informs practice and real-world issues test the validity of abstract ideas.
